## Context and Motivation

Faster R-CNN was a major step forward because it combined region proposals and classification
into one deep learning framework. But despite its accuracy, it had a clear limitation: it could
only draw bounding boxes around objects. A bounding box is useful, but it is a very rough way to
represent object shape. Think about a person riding a bicycle. The bounding box will cover both
the rider and the bike, but it does not tell us which pixels belong to the person and which belong
to the bike.

In many applications, this coarse representation is not enough. Self-driving cars, for example,
need to know the exact shape of pedestrians and other vehicles for safe navigation. In medical
imaging, it is important to mark the precise region of a tumor, not just a rectangle around it.
This is where segmentation comes in. Instead of just detecting objects, segmentation aims to label
each pixel. Semantic segmentation does this at the category level, but it cannot separate multiple
instances of the same class. What we really need is **instance segmentation**: identifying each
object and outlining its pixels individually.

Mask R-CNN was introduced to solve exactly this problem. The idea was to take the strong two stage
framework of Faster R-CNN and extend it by adding a branch that predicts masks for each region of
interest. At the same time, the authors addressed a key weakness of Faster R-CNN: the small
misalignments caused by RoI Pooling. They introduced RoI Align, a simple but crucial change that
made pixel-level mask predictions possible. The result was a model that could not only detect
objects and refine bounding boxes, but also predict accurate binary masks for every object in an
image.

## Architecture Breakdown

Mask R-CNN builds directly on Faster R-CNN, but with some important upgrades that allow it to
predict accurate masks. The overall flow is still two stage: first generate proposals, then
classify them and predict masks. Let us walk through the steps in detail.


### Step 1: Backbone CNN with Feature Pyramid Network

Mask R-CNN uses ResNet as its backbone, but with an important addition: the Feature Pyramid Network
(FPN). The idea of the FPN is to create multi-scale feature maps so that both small and large
objects can be represented effectively. Without the FPN, deep layers of the backbone lose too much
spatial resolution, which makes detecting small objects difficult.


#### How ResNet alone works

Suppose we input an 800 × 800 image into ResNet-50. After each stage of convolution and pooling,
the spatial size shrinks while the number of channels grows:

- After conv2 → 200 × 200 × 256 feature map (stride 4).
- After conv3 → 100 × 100 × 512 feature map (stride 8).
- After conv4 → 50 × 50 × 1024 feature map (stride 16).
- After conv5 → 25 × 25 × 2048 feature map (stride 32).

Now imagine there is a 32 × 32 object in the input image.  
At stride 32, this object shrinks to 32 ÷ 32 = 1 pixel. That is not enough detail to localize it.
On the other hand, the shallow conv2 layer has fine detail, but weaker semantic features. This
mismatch is exactly what the FPN solves.


#### How FPN is built

The FPN introduces a **top-down pathway with lateral connections**. The idea is to combine the
strong semantics of deep layers with the fine resolution of shallow layers.

</figure>


Here is the step-by-step construction:

1. Start with conv5 (25 × 25 × 2048). Use a 1 × 1 convolution to reduce it to 256 channels.
   This becomes **P5**.

2. Upsample P5 by a factor of 2 (25 × 25 → 50 × 50). Add it elementwise to conv4, which is also
   reduced to 256 channels with a 1 × 1 convolution. This forms **P4**:

    $P_4 = W_1 \cdot C_4 + \text{Upsample}(P_5)$

3. Repeat the process: upsample P4 (50 × 50 → 100 × 100) and add it to conv3 (after 1 × 1 conv).
   This forms **P3**.

4. Again: upsample P3 (100 × 100 → 200 × 200) and add it to conv2 (after 1 × 1 conv).
   This forms **P2**.

Each Pi is passed through a 3 × 3 convolution to clean up aliasing artifacts. The final pyramid
has consistent 256 channel feature maps:

- P2 → 200 × 200 × 256
- P3 → 100 × 100 × 256
- P4 → 50 × 50 × 256
- P5 → 25 × 25 × 256


#### Assigning regions to pyramid levels

Once the pyramid is built, the model decides which feature map to use for each region. The level
is chosen based on the size of the region using the formula:

$k = \lfloor k_0 + \log_2 \left( \frac{\sqrt{wh}}{224} \right) \rfloor$

where $w$ and $h$ are the width and height of the region, and $k_0 = 4$ (reference to P4).

Examples:

- A 32 × 32 region → $\sqrt{wh} = 32$. Plugging in gives $k = 2$. Use **P2**.
- A 224 × 224 region → $\sqrt{wh} = 224$. Plugging in gives $k = 4$. Use **P4**.
- A 512 × 512 region → $\sqrt{wh} = 512$. Plugging in gives $k = 5$. Use **P5**.


#### Why this matters

The FPN makes it possible to detect objects of all scales with one unified backbone. Small objects
are processed at higher resolution maps, while large objects use coarser maps with stronger
semantics. The important thing to note is that the FPN is still part of the **backbone**. It does
not generate proposals — that job still belongs to the RPN, which we will cover in Step 2.


### Step 2: Region Proposal Network

The Region Proposal Network in Mask R-CNN works the same way as in Faster R-CNN: a small 3 × 3
sliding window scans over the feature map, and for each spatial location it predicts a set of
anchors. Each anchor is scored with an objectness score (object vs background) and refined with
bounding box deltas.

What changes in Mask R-CNN is that the RPN now operates on **all levels of the FPN pyramid**. For
example, consider the feature maps we just built:

- P2 → 200 × 200 (stride 4)
- P3 → 100 × 100 (stride 8)
- P4 → 50 × 50 (stride 16)
- P5 → 25 × 25 (stride 32)

Anchors are generated at every location in each map. Suppose we use 3 scales and 3 aspect ratios.
That means 9 anchors per location. On P2, with 200 × 200 locations, that is 360,000 anchors. On
P3, with 100 × 100, it is 90,000 anchors, and so on. Across all pyramid levels, the RPN easily
handles half a million anchors in one image.

This might sound expensive, but the convolutional operations are shared, so the cost is
manageable. After scoring and refining, proposals from all pyramid levels are merged. Non Maximum
Suppression is then applied to filter redundant boxes, and the top 1000 to 2000 proposals are
selected for the next stage.

The advantage of this design is clear:  
- Small objects are proposed from high-resolution maps like P2.  
- Large objects are proposed from coarse maps like P5.  

By tying the RPN to the pyramid, Mask R-CNN inherits the multi-scale power of FPN while keeping
the same efficient proposal mechanism as Faster R-CNN.


### Step 3: RoI Align

One of the key contributions of Mask R-CNN is **RoI Align**, which fixes the spatial misalignment
introduced by RoI Pooling. To understand why it is important, let us carefully walk through the
difference.


#### The problem with RoI Pooling

Suppose the RPN proposes a region of interest on the feature map with floating point coordinates:
(15.2, 23.7, 47.8, 68.3). These numbers represent the top-left and bottom-right corners of the
proposal on the feature map.

In **RoI Pooling**, these values are rounded to integers: (15, 24, 48, 68). The region is then
divided into a fixed grid, say 7 × 7, and max pooling is applied in each bin. The issue is that
rounding shifts the boundaries. A pixel shift of even 0.5 in the feature map corresponds to a
16-pixel shift in the input image when the stride is 32. For masks, this tiny shift can mean that
object boundaries no longer line up correctly.


#### How RoI Align fixes it

RoI Align removes rounding and uses **bilinear interpolation** instead of max pooling. Let us work
through an example.

Take the same RoI: (15.2, 23.7, 47.8, 68.3). We want to pool it into a 2 × 2 grid (to keep the
math simple; in practice it is usually 7 × 7).

1. Divide the RoI into bins:  
   - Width = 47.8 – 15.2 = 32.6  
   - Height = 68.3 – 23.7 = 44.6  
   - Each bin is therefore 16.3 × 22.3 in size.

2. For each bin, select four sampling points at evenly spaced fractional coordinates.  
   Example: the top-left bin might include points like (16.1, 25.9), (24.2, 25.9), (16.1, 36.3),
   (24.2, 36.3).

3. Compute the feature value at each fractional coordinate using bilinear interpolation.  
   Suppose (24.2, 25.9) lies between four neighboring pixels with values v1, v2, v3, v4. Then:

   $V(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{4} w_i \cdot v_i$

   where the weights $w_i$ are proportional to the distances from (x, y) to each pixel center.

4. Take the average of the four sampled values to get the output for that bin.

Repeating this for all bins gives a perfectly aligned 2 × 2 feature map (or 7 × 7 in practice).
No rounding, no quantization, just smooth interpolation.


#### Why it matters

For bounding boxes, the difference between RoI Pooling and RoI Align is small. But for masks,
pixel accuracy is critical. Without RoI Align, masks often look jagged and misaligned with the
object edges. With RoI Align, the masks line up smoothly with boundaries, which was essential for
the success of Mask R-CNN.


#### Visual reference

The original Mask R-CNN paper has a simple and clear figure comparing RoI Pooling and RoI Align:  
[Figure 3 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.06870.pdf) shows how quantization misaligns
features and how RoI Align fixes it.



### Step 4: Detection Head

Once RoI Align has converted each proposal into a fixed-size feature map, these features are fed
into the detection head. The detection head is responsible for two tasks: predicting the class of
the object and refining the bounding box coordinates. This part of the architecture is essentially
the same as in Faster R-CNN.



#### Input and shape

Suppose the backbone is ResNet with FPN and we pool each proposal into a 7 × 7 grid. If the FPN
outputs 256 channels, then each RoI after RoI Align has shape 256 × 7 × 7. Flattening this gives
12,544 features per RoI.



#### Fully connected layers

These features are passed through two fully connected layers (often with 1024 or 4096 hidden
units). The network learns abstract, high-level representations of the region that are useful for
classification and box regression.

$\text{RoI feature} \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 7 \times 7}$
$\;\;\rightarrow\;\; \mathbb{R}^{12544}$
$\;\;\rightarrow\;\; \mathbb{R}^{1024}$
$\;\;\rightarrow\;\; \mathbb{R}^{1024}$



#### Classification branch

One branch of the detection head outputs class scores. If there are K object categories, the
classifier outputs K + 1 probabilities (the +1 is for the background class). For example, if
there are 80 classes as in COCO, the classifier outputs an 81-dimensional vector for each RoI.

The highest probability decides the predicted class.



#### Bounding box regression branch

The second branch outputs bounding box adjustments. For each class, it predicts four values:
$\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta w, \Delta h$. These represent how to shift and resize the RoI to
better fit the object. In COCO with 80 classes, this branch outputs 80 × 4 = 320 numbers per RoI.

At inference time, only the regression for the predicted class is used to refine the bounding box.



#### Why keep it this way?

The detection head in Mask R-CNN is deliberately left unchanged from Faster R-CNN. The logic is
simple: Faster R-CNN already gave state-of-the-art results for detection. Instead of reinventing
this part, the authors extended it by adding a **third branch** for masks, which we will cover in
the next step. This modular design made it possible for Mask R-CNN to inherit the strong detection
performance of Faster R-CNN while extending it to segmentation.

### Step 5: Mask Head

The main innovation of Mask R-CNN is the addition of a **mask prediction branch**. This branch runs
in parallel with the detection head and produces a pixel-level binary mask for each object. While
the detection head only outputs class scores and bounding box refinements, the mask head predicts
a much finer representation: the outline of the object itself.



#### Input and shape

After RoI Align, each proposal has a feature map of size 256 × 14 × 14 (the paper increased the
resolution from 7 × 7 in Faster R-CNN to 14 × 14 for more spatial detail). These features are fed
into the mask head.



#### Network structure

The mask head is a small Fully Convolutional Network (FCN). Instead of fully connected layers, it
uses convolutional layers so that spatial information is preserved. A typical structure is:

- 4 convolutional layers, each 3 × 3 with 256 filters.  
- 1 deconvolution (transpose convolution) layer that upsamples by 2×.  
- 1 final 1 × 1 convolution layer to output masks.



#### Output dimensions

If there are K classes, the mask head outputs K binary masks for each RoI. Each mask is 28 × 28
pixels in size. For example, in COCO with 80 classes, the output per RoI is 80 × 28 × 28.

At inference time, only the mask corresponding to the predicted class (from the detection head) is
kept. For example, if the detection head predicts “person” for a proposal, we take only the “person”
mask from the 80 outputs.



#### Training the mask head

The mask branch is trained with a per-pixel **binary cross entropy loss**. For each positive RoI,
the ground truth mask is cropped to the RoI and resized to 28 × 28. Only the mask for the true
class is used in the loss — this prevents competition between classes and makes training more
stable.



#### Why this works

By separating the mask prediction into its own branch, Mask R-CNN keeps the detection head focused
on classification and bounding box refinement, while the mask head specializes in pixel-level
segmentation. This parallel design is efficient because both branches share the same RoI features
from RoI Align. The 28 × 28 mask is coarse compared to the original image, but it can be
upsampled later to the input resolution. Even at this size, it is accurate enough to capture fine
object boundaries.



#### Visual reference

The architecture of the mask head is shown clearly in the original Mask R-CNN paper:  
[Figure 2 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.06870.pdf) illustrates the three parallel
branches — classification, bounding box regression, and mask prediction.


## Training Procedure

Training Mask R-CNN is more involved than Faster R-CNN because it now has three main objectives:
classification, bounding box regression, and mask prediction. The key idea is to optimize all of
them together with a multi-task loss.



#### Step 1: RPN loss

The Region Proposal Network is trained the same way as in Faster R-CNN. At each anchor, the RPN
predicts whether the anchor is foreground (contains an object) or background. The classification
loss is a binary cross entropy, and the regression loss adjusts anchor coordinates with a smooth
L1 loss.

$
L_{\text{RPN}} = L_{\text{cls}}^{\text{RPN}} + L_{\text{reg}}^{\text{RPN}}
$

where  
- $L_{\text{cls}}^{\text{RPN}} = - \big[ y \log p + (1-y)\log(1-p) \big]$
- $L_{\text{reg}}^{\text{RPN}} = \text{smooth}_{L1}(t - t^*)$

Here $y \in \{0,1\}$ is the ground truth label, $p$ is the predicted probability, $t$ are the
predicted box offsets, and $t^*$ are the ground truth offsets.



#### Step 2: Detection head loss

The detection head also has two branches. The classification branch predicts a distribution over
K + 1 categories (K object classes plus background). The loss here is a softmax cross entropy:

$
L_{\text{cls}}^{\text{det}} = - \sum_{c=1}^{K+1} y_c \log p_c
$

where $y_c$ is the ground truth one-hot vector and $p_c$ are the predicted probabilities.

The bounding box regression branch predicts 4 values per class:  
$\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta w, \Delta h$. Only the regression for the true class is used in the
loss, again with smooth L1:

$
L_{\text{reg}}^{\text{det}} = \text{smooth}_{L1}(t - t^*)
$



#### Step 3: Mask loss

The mask branch predicts a binary mask for each class. Suppose the RoI is assigned to class $k$.
Then only the kth mask is used in the loss, while the others are ignored. This prevents the mask
head from wasting capacity on irrelevant classes.

The loss is a pixel-wise binary cross entropy averaged over all pixels in the 28 × 28 mask:

$
L_{\text{mask}} = - \frac{1}{m^2} \sum_{i,j} \big[ y_{ij}\log \hat{y}_{ij} + (1-y_{ij})\log (1-\hat{y}_{ij}) \big]
$

where $m = 28$, $y_{ij}$ is the ground truth mask value at pixel (i,j), and $\hat{y}_{ij}$ is
the predicted probability.



#### Step 4: Final loss

The total loss is simply the sum of all the components:

$
L = L_{\text{RPN}} + L_{\text{cls}}^{\text{det}} + L_{\text{reg}}^{\text{det}} + L_{\text{mask}}
$

This allows the backbone, RPN, detection head, and mask head to be trained end-to-end in one
framework. Gradients flow through all branches, and the shared backbone features are updated in a
way that benefits every task.



#### Training details

- **Mini-batches**: Typically 256 RoIs per image are sampled for the detection head, balanced
  between positive and negative samples.  
- **Mask targets**: Ground truth masks are cropped to the RoI and resized to 28 × 28.  
- **Backbone initialization**: ResNet is usually pretrained on ImageNet before being fine-tuned
  within Mask R-CNN.  
- **Optimization**: SGD with momentum is used, often with learning rate schedules like step decay.  

By training all tasks jointly, Mask R-CNN learns representations that are useful for detection
and segmentation at the same time, without the need for separate networks.


## Implementation Notes

Let us now translate Mask R-CNN into practical steps. We will write down the code structure as if
we were implementing the model from scratch in PyTorch. This helps connect the theory to actual
practice. We start with the backbone and the Feature Pyramid Network (FPN).



### Step 1: Backbone CNN with Feature Pyramid Network

Mask R-CNN uses ResNet as the backbone, often ResNet-50 or ResNet-101. To build an FPN on top of
it, we need to extract feature maps at different stages of the ResNet. These are usually taken
from conv2, conv3, conv4, and conv5. Their strides are 4, 8, 16, and 32 relative to the input
image.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torchvision.models as models

class ResNetBackbone(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, name="resnet50"):
        super().__init__()
        # Load pretrained ResNet
        resnet = getattr(models, name)(pretrained=True)
        
        # Keep only the convolutional layers, discard final avgpool + fc
        # ResNet is structured as: stem -> layer1 -> layer2 -> layer3 -> layer4
        self.stem = nn.Sequential(
            resnet.conv1,  # 7x7 conv
            resnet.bn1,
            resnet.relu,
            resnet.maxpool
        )
        self.layer1 = resnet.layer1  # conv2, stride 4
        self.layer2 = resnet.layer2  # conv3, stride 8
        self.layer3 = resnet.layer3  # conv4, stride 16
        self.layer4 = resnet.layer4  # conv5, stride 32

    def forward(self, x):
        c1 = self.stem(x)    # stride 4
        c2 = self.layer1(c1) # stride 4 (conv2)
        c3 = self.layer2(c2) # stride 8 (conv3)
        c4 = self.layer3(c3) # stride 16 (conv4)
        c5 = self.layer4(c4) # stride 32 (conv5)
        return c2, c3, c4, c5
```

At this point, for an 800 × 800 input image:

- c2 has shape [B, 256, 200, 200]
- c3 has shape [B, 512, 100, 100]
- c4 has shape [B, 1024, 50, 50]
- c5 has shape [B, 2048, 25, 25]

### Building the FPN layers

We now build the top-down pathway with lateral connections. The idea is to reduce each feature
map to 256 channels with a 1×1 convolution, then progressively upsample and add to the next
shallower map.

```python
class FPN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels_list, out_channels=256):
        """
        in_channels_list: [256, 512, 1024, 2048] from ResNet stages
        out_channels: usually 256
        """
        super().__init__()
        # 1x1 conv layers for lateral connections
        self.lateral_convs = nn.ModuleList([
            nn.Conv2d(in_ch, out_channels, kernel_size=1)
            for in_ch in in_channels_list
        ])
        
        # 3x3 conv layers to smooth after merging
        self.output_convs = nn.ModuleList([
            nn.Conv2d(out_channels, out_channels, kernel_size=3, padding=1)
            for _ in in_channels_list
        ])

    def forward(self, inputs):
        # inputs: [c2, c3, c4, c5]
        c2, c3, c4, c5 = inputs
        
        # Step 1: reduce c5 to 256 channels
        p5 = self.lateral_convs[3](c5)
        
        # Step 2: build P4 by upsampling P5 and adding to c4
        p4 = self.lateral_convs[2](c4) + nn.functional.interpolate(
            p5, size=c4.shape[-2:], mode="nearest"
        )
        
        # Step 3: build P3
        p3 = self.lateral_convs[1](c3) + nn.functional.interpolate(
            p4, size=c3.shape[-2:], mode="nearest"
        )
        
        # Step 4: build P2
        p2 = self.lateral_convs[0](c2) + nn.functional.interpolate(
            p3, size=c2.shape[-2:], mode="nearest"
        )
        
        # Final 3x3 conv to smooth
        p5 = self.output_convs[3](p5)
        p4 = self.output_convs[2](p4)
        p3 = self.output_convs[1](p3)
        p2 = self.output_convs[0](p2)
        
        return p2, p3, p4, p5
```

### Checking the shapes

If the input image is 800 × 800, the output pyramid maps are:

- P2 → [B, 256, 200, 200]
- P3 → [B, 256, 100, 100]
- P4 → [B, 256, 50, 50]
- P5 → [B, 256, 25, 25]

All of them have the same depth (256 channels) but different resolutions. These maps are now ready
to be used by the RPN in the next step.

Example usage:
```python
backbone = ResNetBackbone("resnet50")
fpn = FPN([256, 512, 1024, 2048], 256)

x = torch.randn(1, 3, 800, 800)  # batch of 1 image
c2, c3, c4, c5 = backbone(x)
p2, p3, p4, p5 = fpn([c2, c3, c4, c5])

print(p2.shape, p3.shape, p4.shape, p5.shape)
# torch.Size([1, 256, 200, 200])
# torch.Size([1, 256, 100, 100])
# torch.Size([1, 256, 50, 50])
# torch.Size([1, 256, 25, 25])
```

This matches exactly what we expect from the FPN construction we described earlier.

### Step 2: Region Proposal Network (RPN)

The Region Proposal Network is applied on each FPN level (P2, P3, P4, P5). At every location on
these maps, the RPN places multiple **anchors** of different scales and aspect ratios. For each
anchor, it predicts two things: whether it contains an object (objectness score) and how to refine
its coordinates (box regression deltas).



### RPN Head

The RPN head is small and fully convolutional. It has three parts:

1. A 3×3 convolution that processes the feature map at each location.  
2. A classification layer that predicts foreground vs background for every anchor.  
3. A regression layer that predicts four offsets (Δx, Δy, Δw, Δh) for every anchor.

```python
class RPNHead(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels, num_anchors):
        super().__init__()
        self.conv = nn.Conv2d(in_channels, 256, kernel_size=3, padding=1)
        self.cls_logits = nn.Conv2d(256, num_anchors * 2, kernel_size=1)
        self.bbox_pred = nn.Conv2d(256, num_anchors * 4, kernel_size=1)

    def forward(self, x):
        # x: [B, 256, H, W]
        t = nn.functional.relu(self.conv(x))
        logits = self.cls_logits(t)    # [B, num_anchors*2, H, W]
        bbox = self.bbox_pred(t)       # [B, num_anchors*4, H, W]
        return logits, bbox
```

If we use 3 scales × 3 aspect ratios = 9 anchors per location, then:
- cls_logits outputs 18 channels (9 anchors × 2 classes).
- bbox_pred outputs 36 channels (9 anchors × 4 coords).

### Anchor generation

For every location in each pyramid map, we generate anchors. Each anchor is centered at the pixel
location, with predefined scales and aspect ratios. Let’s write a generator.

```python
import itertools
import torch

def generate_anchors(base_size, scales, ratios, feat_h, feat_w, stride):
    anchors = []
    for y, x in itertools.product(range(feat_h), range(feat_w)):
        center_x, center_y = x * stride, y * stride
        for scale in scales:
            for ratio in ratios:
                w = scale * (ratio ** 0.5)
                h = scale / (ratio ** 0.5)
                anchors.append([
                    center_x - w / 2, center_y - h / 2,
                    center_x + w / 2, center_y + h / 2
                ])
    return torch.tensor(anchors)  # [N, 4]
```

### Example: Number of anchors

On P3 (stride = 8, feature map size 100 × 100), with 9 anchors per location:

$
100 \times 100 \times 9 = 90{,}000 \; \text{anchors}
$

Across all pyramid levels P2–P5, this easily adds up to more than 500,000 anchors per image.



### Bounding box decoding

The RPN outputs regression deltas that adjust anchors into refined boxes.  
Given an anchor $(x_a, y_a, w_a, h_a)$ and predicted deltas $(t_x, t_y, t_w, t_h)$, the refined
box is computed as:

$
x = x_a + t_x \cdot w_a
$

$
y = y_a + t_y \cdot h_a
$

$
w = w_a \cdot e^{t_w}
$

$
h = h_a \cdot e^{t_h}
$

The final box coordinates are then reconstructed from the new center $(x, y)$ and dimensions
$(w, h)$.

```python
def apply_deltas_to_anchors(anchors, deltas):
    # anchors: [N, 4], deltas: [N, 4]
    xa = (anchors[:, 0] + anchors[:, 2]) / 2
    ya = (anchors[:, 1] + anchors[:, 3]) / 2
    wa = anchors[:, 2] - anchors[:, 0]
    ha = anchors[:, 3] - anchors[:, 1]

    tx, ty, tw, th = deltas[:, 0], deltas[:, 1], deltas[:, 2], deltas[:, 3]

    x = xa + tx * wa
    y = ya + ty * ha
    w = wa * torch.exp(tw)
    h = ha * torch.exp(th)

    x1 = x - w / 2
    y1 = y - h / 2
    x2 = x + w / 2
    y2 = y + h / 2

    return torch.stack([x1, y1, x2, y2], dim=1)
```

### Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS)

After generating boxes, many overlap. NMS is applied to keep the top-scoring boxes and remove
redundant ones.

```python
from torchvision.ops import nms

def filter_proposals(boxes, scores, nms_thresh=0.7, top_n=2000):
    keep = nms(boxes, scores, iou_threshold=nms_thresh)
    keep = keep[:top_n]  # keep top N proposals
    return boxes[keep], scores[keep]
```

### Putting it together

For each pyramid map P2–P5:

- Generate anchors for that map.
- Pass the map through the RPN head → get logits and deltas.
- Decode deltas into refined boxes.
- Apply NMS.
- Collect top proposals.

The proposals from all pyramid levels are then merged and passed to RoI Align.

This completes Step 2. The RPN, when combined with FPN, gives a balanced set of proposals for
objects of all sizes - small objects from P2, large objects from P5.

### Step 3: RoI Align

RoI Align replaces RoI Pooling to fix spatial misalignment. The key difference is that instead of
rounding proposal coordinates to integers, RoI Align uses the exact floating-point values and
samples the features with bilinear interpolation.



#### The problem with RoI Pooling

Suppose the RPN proposes a region of interest with coordinates:

$
(x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2) = (15.2, 23.7, 47.8, 68.3)
$

RoI Pooling rounds these to integers:

$
(15, 24, 48, 68)
$

This rounding shifts the region boundaries. With a stride of 32 in the backbone, a 0.5 pixel shift
at the feature map level can mean a 16-pixel misalignment in the input image. This is catastrophic
for mask prediction, where every pixel matters.



#### How RoI Align works

Let us say we want to pool this region into a 2 × 2 output (simplified from the usual 7 × 7).

1. The RoI width and height are:

$
w = 47.8 - 15.2 = 32.6, \quad h = 68.3 - 23.7 = 44.6
$

Each bin is therefore $w/2 = 16.3$ by $h/2 = 22.3$.

2. In the top-left bin, choose four sampling points at fractional coordinates, for example:

$
(16.1, 25.9), (24.2, 25.9), (16.1, 36.3), (24.2, 36.3)
$

3. For each sampling point, compute the feature value with bilinear interpolation.  
   If a point $(x, y)$ falls between four neighboring pixels with values
   $v_{11}, v_{12}, v_{21}, v_{22}$, then:

$
V(x,y) = w_{11}v_{11} + w_{12}v_{12} + w_{21}v_{21} + w_{22}v_{22}
$

where the weights $w_{ij}$ are proportional to the distance from $(x,y)$ to each pixel center.

4. Average the sampled values to get the bin output. Repeat for all bins.

The result is a 2 × 2 feature map (or 7 × 7 in practice) that is perfectly aligned with the RoI,
without quantization.



#### PyTorch-style implementation

PyTorch provides `roi_align` in `torchvision.ops`, which implements exactly this idea.

```python
from torchvision.ops import roi_align

# feature_map: [B, C, H, W]
# rois: tensor of shape [num_rois, 5] where each row is [batch_idx, x1, y1, x2, y2]
pooled = roi_align(
    feature_map, rois,
    output_size=(7, 7),   # pooled to 7x7
    spatial_scale=1/16,   # if backbone stride = 16
    sampling_ratio=2      # number of samples per bin
)
```

#### Why it matters

For bounding box detection, the rounding error of RoI Pooling caused only small accuracy drops.
But for masks, even a few pixels of misalignment ruin the output. RoI Align solved this issue,
making instance segmentation practical for the first time.


### Step 4: Detection Head

The detection head is very similar to Faster R-CNN. After RoI Align, each proposal is represented
as a fixed feature map (for example 256 × 7 × 7). These features are flattened and fed into fully
connected layers, which branch into classification and bounding box regression outputs.



#### Implementation

```python
class DetectionHead(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels, num_classes, roi_size=7):
        super().__init__()
        # Flattened feature size = in_channels * roi_size * roi_size
        self.fc1 = nn.Linear(in_channels * roi_size * roi_size, 1024)
        self.fc2 = nn.Linear(1024, 1024)

        # Classification branch: predicts class scores
        self.cls_score = nn.Linear(1024, num_classes + 1)  # +1 for background

        # Regression branch: predicts box deltas
        self.bbox_pred = nn.Linear(1024, num_classes * 4)

    def forward(self, x):
        x = x.flatten(start_dim=1)
        x = nn.functional.relu(self.fc1(x))
        x = nn.functional.relu(self.fc2(x))

        cls_logits = self.cls_score(x)   # [N, num_classes+1]
        bbox_deltas = self.bbox_pred(x) # [N, num_classes*4]
        return cls_logits, bbox_deltas
```

#### Example

If we have 80 object categories (COCO dataset):

- Classification outputs 81 scores per RoI (80 classes + background).
- Regression outputs 320 values per RoI (80 classes × 4 deltas).

### Step 5: Mask Head

The mask head is the unique addition in Mask R-CNN. Instead of fully connected layers, it uses
convolutions to preserve spatial structure. It takes higher-resolution RoI features (14 × 14) and
outputs a binary mask for each class at 28 × 28 resolution.

#### Implementation
```python
class MaskHead(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels, num_classes, roi_size=14):
        super().__init__()
        # Four 3x3 convolutional layers
        layers = []
        for _ in range(4):
            layers.append(nn.Conv2d(in_channels, 256, kernel_size=3, padding=1))
            layers.append(nn.ReLU(inplace=True))
        self.convs = nn.Sequential(*layers)

        # Upsample with a transpose convolution
        self.deconv = nn.ConvTranspose2d(256, 256, kernel_size=2, stride=2)

        # Final 1x1 conv to output K masks of size 28x28
        self.mask_pred = nn.Conv2d(256, num_classes, kernel_size=1)

    def forward(self, x):
        # Input: [N, C, 14, 14] after RoI Align
        x = self.convs(x)
        x = nn.functional.relu(self.deconv(x))  # [N, 256, 28, 28]
        masks = self.mask_pred(x)               # [N, num_classes, 28, 28]
        return masks
```

#### Training

- For each positive RoI, only the mask corresponding to its ground truth class is used in the loss.
- The loss is a pixel-wise binary cross entropy across the 28 × 28 mask.
- This avoids competition between classes and makes training more stable.

Example

If we detect a “person” in an RoI:

- The detection head predicts class = “person”.
- The mask head outputs 80 masks (one for each class in COCO).
- We select only the “person” mask (28 × 28).
- The mask is resized to the RoI’s bounding box and placed back into the image.

## Limitations

Mask R-CNN was a huge leap forward, but it was not without trade-offs. The first limitation was
**speed**. Even with the RPN and shared backbone, it remained a two-stage detector. Running RoI
Align, a detection head, and a mask head for thousands of proposals made it too slow for real-time
applications like robotics or live video.

The second limitation was **computational cost**. The mask branch, with its extra convolutions,
added significant memory and compute requirements. On large datasets like COCO, training Mask R-CNN
required multiple GPUs and long training schedules. Deploying it on edge devices was not practical.

The third limitation was **small object segmentation**. While the FPN improved small object
detection, segmenting tiny objects remained a challenge. A 28 × 28 mask is already coarse, and when
upsampled back to the original resolution, the fine details can get lost. Thin structures such as
bicycle spokes or tree branches often came out blurry.

Finally, like Faster R-CNN, it was still somewhat complex to train. The multi-task loss, balancing
between RPN, classification, bounding box regression, and masks, required careful tuning.



## Reflections

When I first studied Mask R-CNN, what impressed me was how *natural* the extension felt. The
authors did not try to reinvent object detection. They simply took the successful Faster R-CNN
framework and asked, “what if we add one more branch for masks.” This simplicity is what made it so
powerful. By keeping the detection head unchanged and only adding the mask head, the model became
modular, easy to extend, and still very accurate.

The introduction of RoI Align was also an important lesson. Sometimes small design decisions, like
whether to round coordinates or interpolate them, can make a massive difference. Bounding boxes
were tolerant to the quantization error of RoI Pooling, but masks were not. RoI Align fixed this
and made the whole model practical.

For me, Mask R-CNN represents the point where deep learning in computer vision stopped being just
about “detecting things” and started being about *understanding them in detail*. The ability to
say not only “there is a person” but also “these are the exact pixels of the person” opened up
fields like panoptic segmentation, DensePose, and keypoint detection. It showed how modular design
lets us build more and more complex vision systems on top of the same foundations.

My main takeaway is that progress in computer vision often comes from asking simple questions. Faster
R-CNN solved region proposals, and Mask R-CNN asked: what if we also predict masks. That single step
created one of the most widely used baselines in research and industry.
