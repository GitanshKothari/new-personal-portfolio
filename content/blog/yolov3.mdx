## Context, Motivation, and Problems Solved

YOLOv2 fixed many of the problems from YOLOv1 by introducing anchors, a stronger backbone, and
multi-scale training. It was faster and more accurate, and YOLO9000 showed that detection could be
scaled to thousands of classes. But even with these improvements, there were still a few important
limitations.

The first issue was with **small object detection**. YOLOv2 introduced a passthrough layer to bring
higher-resolution features into the detection head, but it was still not enough. Small objects like
birds, bottles, or distant traffic signs often went undetected because the grid was too coarse.

The second issue was with **classification**. YOLOv2 used a softmax across classes, which forced
each bounding box to pick exactly one category. But in real-world data, labels can overlap (for
example, “dog” and “animal”). A softmax also assumes classes are mutually exclusive, which is not
always the case.

The third issue was with the **backbone depth**. Darknet-19 was efficient, but it was not as deep
or powerful as modern feature extractors like ResNet. This limited YOLOv2’s ability to learn rich,
multi-level representations.

YOLOv3, introduced in 2018, was designed to address these problems. It introduced a new backbone
(Darknet-53) with residual connections, used **multi-scale detection** inspired by feature pyramid
networks, and replaced softmax classification with **independent logistic classifiers**. These
changes made YOLOv3 much stronger on small objects and multi-label scenarios, while still running
at real-time speeds.

## Architecture Breakdown

### Step 1: Backbone (Darknet-53)

YOLOv3 replaced the Darknet-19 backbone from YOLOv2 with a much deeper and more powerful network
called **Darknet-53**. This was a critical upgrade that gave YOLOv3 its boost in accuracy.

---

#### Darknet-53 design

Darknet-53 has **53 convolutional layers** organized with **residual connections**, very similar to
ResNet. The key design choices:

- Only 3×3 and 1×1 convolutions, no fully connected layers.  
- Batch normalization and Leaky ReLU after every convolution.  
- Residual (skip) connections to ease training of deeper networks.  
- Global average pooling for classification when pretrained on ImageNet.  

---

#### Comparison with Darknet-19

On ImageNet classification:

- Darknet-19: 72.9% top-1 accuracy, 171 FPS.  
- Darknet-53: 77.2% top-1 accuracy, 78 FPS.  

This meant YOLOv3 traded some speed for significantly richer features. The residual connections
helped the deeper backbone avoid vanishing gradients and capture more semantic detail.

---

#### Example feature map flow

For a 416 × 416 input:

- After initial conv + pooling → 208 × 208 feature map.  
- Deeper layers reduce resolution progressively → 104 × 104, 52 × 52, 26 × 26, 13 × 13.  
- These multi-scale feature maps are reused later in the detection head.

---

#### Why this matters

YOLOv3’s backbone was now competitive with ResNet-50 and ResNet-101 in accuracy, while still being
much faster. This gave YOLOv3 the strong foundation it needed to improve detection without losing
its signature speed advantage.

### Step 2: Multi-Scale Detection

One of the most important changes in YOLOv3 was adding **multi-scale detection**, inspired by
Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN). Instead of detecting objects only at the final feature map
(13 × 13), YOLOv3 predicted boxes at **three different scales**: 13 × 13, 26 × 26, and 52 × 52.

---

#### How it works

- **13 × 13 feature map** (stride 32): best for large objects.  
- **26 × 26 feature map** (stride 16): handles medium objects.  
- **52 × 52 feature map** (stride 8): captures small objects.  

Each scale predicts boxes using its own set of anchors. The feature maps are built by combining
higher-level semantic features with lower-level fine-grained features, giving the network a pyramid
of representations.

---

#### Example with 416 × 416 input

- At 13 × 13 scale: each grid cell covers 32 × 32 pixels.  
- At 26 × 26 scale: each grid cell covers 16 × 16 pixels.  
- At 52 × 52 scale: each grid cell covers 8 × 8 pixels.  

So a small object that might have been lost in YOLOv2’s 13 × 13 map could now be represented across
multiple cells in the 52 × 52 map.

---

#### Output at each scale

For each grid cell and anchor, YOLOv3 predicts:

$
(x, y, w, h, objectness) + \\text\{C class scores\}
$

- COCO dataset (C = 80, B = 3 anchors per scale):  
  Output per scale = S × S × (3 × (5 + 80))  

- At 13 × 13: 13 × 13 × 255 = 43,845 values.  
- At 26 × 26: 26 × 26 × 255 = 173,940 values.  
- At 52 × 52: 52 × 52 × 255 = 695,520 values.  

Together, these outputs allow YOLOv3 to detect objects at all scales.

---

#### Why this matters

YOLOv2 struggled with small objects because its detection happened only at a coarse 13 × 13 grid.
By introducing predictions at 26 × 26 and 52 × 52, YOLOv3 greatly improved recall for smaller
objects while maintaining performance on medium and large ones.

### Step 3: Predictions per Anchor Box

YOLOv3 kept the anchor-based prediction idea from YOLOv2 but made two key improvements:  
1. **Independent logistic classifiers** instead of softmax.  
2. Better parameterization for objectness and class scores.

---

#### Predictions per anchor

For each anchor at each grid cell, YOLOv3 predicts:

- $t_x, t_y, t_w, t_h$: offsets for the bounding box.  
- $t_o$: objectness score.  
- $t_c$: C class scores (logits).  

These are transformed into usable values as follows:

$
b_x = \sigma(t_x) + c_x, \quad b_y = \sigma(t_y) + c_y
$

$
b_w = p_w \cdot e^{t_w}, \quad b_h = p_h \cdot e^{t_h}
$

$
\text{objectness} = \sigma(t_o)
$

$
\text{class probability for class i} = \sigma(t_{c_i})
$

---

#### Logistic regression instead of softmax

YOLOv2 used a softmax across all classes, forcing each prediction to belong to exactly one class.
But in the real world, labels can overlap (for example, a “dog” is also an “animal”). Softmax is
too restrictive in such cases.

YOLOv3 switched to **independent logistic classifiers** for each class. That means each class gets
its own binary prediction:

$
\Pr(\text{class i} \mid \text{object}) = \sigma(t_{c_i})
$

This allows multi-label predictions and makes the classification task more flexible.

---

#### Why this matters

This change made YOLOv3 better at handling overlapping categories and reduced classification errors
caused by softmax competition. Combined with anchors and multi-scale detection, it gave YOLOv3 a
much more reliable prediction head than its predecessors.


### Step 4: Output Tensor Sizes

Now that we know YOLOv3 predicts at three scales (13 × 13, 26 × 26, 52 × 52) and uses 3 anchors per
scale, let’s look at the full output tensor sizes.

---

#### Formula

For each scale, the prediction tensor has shape:

$
S \times S \times (B \times (5 + C))
$

where:  
- $S$: grid size (13, 26, or 52).  
- $B$: number of anchors per scale (3).  
- $C$: number of classes (20 for VOC, 80 for COCO).  
- 5 values = (x, y, w, h, objectness).  

---

#### Example: COCO (80 classes)

Each anchor predicts:  
$
5 + 80 = 85 \; \text{values}
$

With 3 anchors per grid cell:  
$
3 \times 85 = 255 \; \text{values per cell}
$

So:  
- At 13 × 13: 13 × 13 × 255 = 43,845 values.  
- At 26 × 26: 26 × 26 × 255 = 173,940 values.  
- At 52 × 52: 52 × 52 × 255 = 695,520 values.  

Total = **913,305 values per image**.

---

#### Example: Pascal VOC (20 classes)

Each anchor predicts:  
$
5 + 20 = 25
$

With 3 anchors per cell:  
$
3 \times 25 = 75
$

So:  
- At 13 × 13: 13 × 13 × 75 = 12,675 values.  
- At 26 × 26: 26 × 26 × 75 = 50,700 values.  
- At 52 × 52: 52 × 52 × 75 = 202,800 values.  

Total = **266,175 values per image**.

---

#### Why this matters

YOLOv3 outputs a **lot more predictions** than YOLOv1 or YOLOv2, but in a structured way: three
different scales, each with anchors tailored to object sizes. This dramatically improved recall,
especially for small objects, while keeping computation efficient enough for real-time inference.



## Training Procedure

YOLOv3 followed a similar training pipeline to YOLOv2 but introduced a few key differences: a
deeper backbone, logistic classifiers for classes, and stronger emphasis on multi-scale detection.

---

### Step 1: Pretraining on ImageNet

As with YOLOv2, the backbone (Darknet-53) was first pretrained on ImageNet for classification.  

- Input: 256 × 256, later fine-tuned at 416 × 416.  
- Top-1 accuracy: 77.2%.  
- Pretraining gave the network a strong initialization, ensuring stable detection training.  

---

### Step 2: Detection fine-tuning

After pretraining, the backbone was fine-tuned on detection datasets (Pascal VOC, COCO).  

- Input resolution: default 416 × 416, but varied with multi-scale training.  
- Optimizer: SGD with momentum (0.9), weight decay (0.0005).  
- Learning rate schedule: warmup at 0.001, decayed stepwise during training.  
- Batch size: commonly 64.  

---

### Step 3: Loss function

YOLOv3’s loss function had three parts, similar to YOLOv2 but adapted for logistic classifiers:  

1. **Localization loss**  
   - Squared error for offsets $(t_x, t_y, t_w, t_h)$.  
   - Each anchor responsible for the object with the highest IoU.  

2. **Objectness loss**  
   - Binary cross-entropy loss for objectness:  
   $
   L_{obj} = - \big[ y \log(\sigma(t_o)) + (1-y)\log(1-\sigma(t_o)) \big]
   $  
   where $y = 1$ if object is present, else 0.  

3. **Classification loss**  
   - Binary cross-entropy for each class, since YOLOv3 uses independent logistic classifiers:  
   $
   L_{cls} = \sum_{c=1}^C - \big[ y_c \log(\sigma(t_{c})) + (1-y_c)\log(1-\sigma(t_c)) \big]
   $  

The total loss was the sum of these three components.  

---

### Step 4: Multi-scale training

Just like YOLOv2, YOLOv3 trained on multiple resolutions to make it robust at inference.  

- Every 10 batches, input resolution was randomly picked from 320 to 608 in steps of 32.  
- This allowed the same weights to work at different trade-offs between speed and accuracy.  

---

### Why this matters

YOLOv3’s training procedure was carefully designed to maximize flexibility. Pretraining gave strong
features, logistic classification fixed YOLOv2’s issues with softmax, and multi-scale training
ensured robustness across different input sizes. This made YOLOv3 significantly more accurate while
still retaining real-time performance.


## Implementation Notes

YOLOv3 is more complex than YOLOv1 and YOLOv2 because it predicts at three different scales, but
the logic is the same: anchors + offsets → bounding boxes. Let’s break it down step by step.

---

### Step 1: Output tensor with 3 scales

At each scale (13 × 13, 26 × 26, 52 × 52), YOLOv3 predicts using **3 anchors per cell**.  
Each anchor outputs:

$
(x, y, w, h, objectness) + C \; \text{class scores}
$

So for COCO (C = 80):

- Per anchor: 85 values.  
- Per cell: 3 × 85 = 255 values.  

That gives:

- 13 × 13 × 255 = 43,845 values.  
- 26 × 26 × 255 = 173,940 values.  
- 52 × 52 × 255 = 695,520 values.  

Total = **913,305 values per image**.

---

### Step 2: Decoding predictions

The outputs are decoded similarly to YOLOv2, but applied at three scales.  

For each anchor at cell (c_x, c_y):

$
b_x = \frac{1}{S}(\sigma(t_x) + c_x), \quad b_y = \frac{1}{S}(\sigma(t_y) + c_y)
$

$
b_w = \frac{1}{W}(p_w \cdot e^{t_w}), \quad b_h = \frac{1}{H}(p_h \cdot e^{t_h})
$

$
\text{objectness} = \sigma(t_o)
$

$
\text{class probability} = \sigma(t_{c_i})
$

- S = grid size (13, 26, or 52).  
- W, H = input image dimensions.  
- Anchors are chosen separately for each scale (small anchors for 52 × 52, large for 13 × 13).  

---

### Step 3: Training loop

The training loop is almost the same as YOLOv2, with the addition of multi-scale predictions.  

for epoch in range(num_epochs):
    for batch_idx, (images, targets) in enumerate(dataloader):
        if batch_idx % 10 == 0:
            new_dim = random.choice([320,352,384,416,448,480,512,544,576,608])
            dataloader.set_input_dim(new_dim)

        preds_13, preds_26, preds_52 = model(images)

        loss_13 = criterion(preds_13, targets)
        loss_26 = criterion(preds_26, targets)
        loss_52 = criterion(preds_52, targets)

        total_loss = loss_13 + loss_26 + loss_52
        total_loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
        optimizer.zero_grad()

---

### Why this matters

The multi-scale outputs and logistic classification made YOLOv3’s implementation more complicated
than earlier YOLO versions. But once broken down, the logic is consistent: anchors are decoded into
bounding boxes, predictions are trained across multiple scales, and all outputs are combined during
loss computation. This design gave YOLOv3 its strength without abandoning real-time performance.


## Limitations

YOLOv3 was a big leap forward compared to YOLOv2, but it still had some limitations. The first
issue was that its **accuracy on small objects** still lagged behind two-stage detectors like Faster
R-CNN with FPN. The 52 × 52 scale helped, but objects smaller than 16 × 16 pixels were often missed
or poorly localized.

Another limitation was the **model size and speed**. Darknet-53 was much deeper than Darknet-19,
which improved accuracy but reduced FPS. On a Titan X GPU, YOLOv2 ran at about 67 FPS, while
YOLOv3 dropped closer to 45 FPS. This was still real-time, but the trade-off meant YOLOv3 wasn’t as
lightweight for mobile or embedded systems.

A third limitation was that YOLOv3 was still **anchor-based**, meaning it required k-means anchor
clustering and hand-tuned priors. This made the system more complex and less adaptive compared to
later anchor-free detectors like CenterNet or FCOS.

Finally, YOLOv3 did not include tricks like attention mechanisms or advanced feature fusion, which
would later become common in YOLOv4 and beyond.

---

## Reflections

For me, YOLOv3 feels like the first “mature” YOLO. Where YOLOv1 was raw and YOLOv2 was polished,
YOLOv3 hit the sweet spot: strong backbone, multi-scale detection, logistic classifiers, and
real-time performance. It showed that one-stage detectors could seriously compete with two-stage
models, not just in speed but also in accuracy.

YOLOv3 also influenced the field in subtle but important ways. Its use of residual connections
through Darknet-53 mirrored the success of ResNets. Its multi-scale detection popularized ideas
similar to FPNs in the single-stage world. And its switch to independent logistic classifiers
foreshadowed modern detectors that handle multi-label outputs.

In many ways, YOLOv3 set the template for all YOLO versions that followed. YOLOv4 and YOLOv5 built
on these ideas, adding more tricks and optimizations, but the core blueprint — multi-scale anchors,
residual backbones, and logistic classification — came from YOLOv3. That’s why it’s remembered as
the version where YOLO went from being “fast and fun” to being **state of the art**.
